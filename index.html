About Me

Linking phenotype, genetics, and molecular mechanisms under combined stress.

I am a plant scientist and Postdoctoral Research Associate in Plant Systems Biology, Biosciences Division, at Oak Ridge National Laboratory. My research investigates how trees and crops respond to combined abiotic stresses under climate change.

My work combines RGB imaging, chlorophyll fluorescence, thermal imaging, VNIR/SWIR hyperspectral imaging, plant physiology, stress-biomarker analysis, population genomics, genome-wide association studies, transcriptomics, metabolomics, and proteomics.

Research includes Populus species and crops such as soybean, wheat, barley, chilli, tomato, cotton, and sorghum.

Research areas

Read the animation transcript

What if future crops could grow with much less synthetic fertilizer? Nitrogen is one of the most important nutrients for plant growth. Farmers often use nitrogen fertilizer to support crop yields, but fertilizer can be expensive and energy-intensive to produce. Nature already provides an extraordinary example. Certain microbes can convert nitrogen gas from the air into forms plants can use. Legumes, such as beans, peas, and clover, often form partnerships with these microbes around their roots. Scientists are exploring ways to improve natural nitrogen fixation and nitrogen-use efficiency in crops. If successful, these approaches could reduce fertilizer demand and support more sustainable food production.
